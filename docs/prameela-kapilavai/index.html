A lifelong voice of faith and reflection

Prameela Kapilavai

Rooted in India’s epics and spiritual traditions, her writing reflects on faith, dharma, relationships and the quiet complexities of human character.

A Life of Learning, Sacrifice, Faith and Quiet Strength

There is something deeply emotional about writing about your own mother. I have written about people, ideas, technology, astrology and many things that matter to me, but writing this page feels different. Perhaps it is because no number of words can quite hold the life of someone who has been woven into almost every part of your own. Prameela Kapilavai is my mother, but she is also one of the strongest, most intelligent, most loving and deeply grounded women I have known. Whatever I write here will inevitably be less than what she has been to our family and less than what she means to me.

My mother worked in the Adult Education Department and served as a Project Officer, spending more than thirty years at the Hyderabad Collectorate. During those thirty plus years, she repeatedly declined promotions because accepting them would have required her to leave Hyderabad. She had the ability and the opportunity to rise much further in her career, but advancement repeatedly presented her with the same choice: move away and disrupt the life of the family, or remain where my father, my sister and I could continue with the stability we had. Again and again, she chose us. Even after declining so many opportunities over the years, had she continued in service instead of taking voluntary retirement, she would have retired at the level of a Director.

Looking back now, I understand that this was not one dramatic sacrifice made on one particular day. It was a decision she made repeatedly over more than three decades. Every promotion she declined represented another possibility for her own career that she quietly allowed to pass because she did not want her professional growth to come at the cost of uprooting our family. The career she could have had was much larger than the one she allowed herself to pursue, and the distance between those two lives is, in many ways, what she gave to my father, my sister and me. As a child I could never have understood the weight of that. As a woman with a career of my own, I understand it very differently today.

About fifteen years ago, after more than three decades of service, she chose voluntary retirement when a transfer made commuting from Hyderabad difficult and health concerns made continuing impractical. By then, she had already given more than thirty years to her work while quietly making countless choices that kept our family together. Retirement marked the end of her professional career, but not the discipline, responsibility and sense of duty with which she had lived it.

There is one childhood memory that feels especially tender to me now. When I was around eight or nine, I used to become very upset that my mother could not come to school and pick me up at three in the afternoon. She was working, but at that age I did not understand the demands of her job or the responsibilities she was carrying. I only knew that I wanted my mother there, and in the absolute logic of a child, I would sometimes tell her that she did not love me because she could not come to pick me up.

Today those arguments are among the memories that make me smile and ache at the same time. I can see the situation with an adult's eyes now. I was accusing a woman of not loving me while she was spending her entire life making choices for precisely the people she loved. Yet my mother being my mother, she apparently remembers every such comment. Even today she can become emotional about the time she believes work took away from my sister and me. She still carries guilt that she could not always be there in the ways she wanted to be, and sometimes she becomes teary thinking about it. That moves me deeply because, from where I stand now, I do not see a mother who failed to give enough. I see a woman who gave so much that she somehow still worries it was not enough.

That instinct to give has never changed. Even today, when her daughters are grown and perfectly capable of taking care of themselves, her mind still works as though every little thing she saves can somehow be preserved for us. She can look at an entire menu and decide that an idli is enough because it is the cheapest tiffin. We can tell her that she worked for decades, that she deserves to order whatever she likes, and that her daughters certainly do not need her to save the price difference between one breakfast and another. It makes very little difference. Somewhere in her mind, apparently, that money still belongs more naturally to her children than to herself. A mother's children grow up. A mother's instinct to save something for them apparently does not.

At home, however, she was always simply Amma to us, which meant my sister and I sometimes forgot how accomplished she was outside the house. My mother is an exceptionally good orator. During her professional years, she shared stages with collectors, ministers and senior officials, and she could speak with an eloquence and confidence that seemed completely natural to her. Whenever we happened to see that side of her, whether at her office or at a program, we would suddenly look at this articulate and commanding woman and think, with genuine surprise, “Oh my God, our mother is actually very impressive.”

That newfound respect usually lasted about three days. Soon enough, my sister and I would return to teasing her and confidently informing her that she did not know this or did not understand that, while quite possibly speaking to the most intelligent person in our family. I think that is one of the privileges children take for granted. The rest of the world may see someone's intelligence, accomplishments and presence. To her children, she remains Amma, the person they feel entirely entitled to argue with about everything.

Books have been part of my mother's life for as long as I can remember. She has been an avid reader from a very young age, and spiritual literature has occupied a particularly important place in her life. Her engagement with spirituality began in her twenties and continued through decades of reading, reflection, study and traditional practice. Her first spiritual guru was my grandmother, Annapoorna Guntur, who was also her mother in law. My grandmother's guru was Ramanatham garu, and my mother later also learned from Ramanatham garu and regarded him as a guru. Spiritual learning was therefore not something distant from family life for her. It was present within the family and became part of the foundation on which her own spiritual understanding developed.

For my mother, spirituality has never been something discovered after retirement or something reserved for particular occasions. It has been part of the rhythm of her everyday life for decades. Puja, madi, acharam and traditional observances have remained part of her life even today. But her spiritual engagement has never been ritual alone. She reads extensively, thinks deeply about what she reads and reflects on the people, choices and moral conflicts within India's epics. Questions of dharma, faith, relationships, human character, suffering and responsibility are not merely subjects she encounters in books. They are things she has spent a lifetime thinking about.

Even when she travels to the United States to stay with me, precious luggage space somehow gets filled with spiritual books she wants to read while she is here. Not one small book for the journey, but books she has decided are important enough to cross continents with her. For my mother, spiritual literature somehow qualifies as necessary international baggage, and by now I have accepted that books will always compete very successfully with clothes for space in her suitcase.

Books are also something my mother and I share, although our reading journeys are our own. I became an avid reader in my own right from my teenage years, constantly reading whatever subjects captured my curiosity, while she had already built her own lifelong relationship with books long before that. Perhaps that is one of the quiet similarities between us. We may choose different subjects and approach them differently, but neither of us has ever needed much convincing to open another book.

My mother was also never the kind of mother who believed that love meant allowing her children to do whatever they wanted. She was strict, sometimes very strict, and my sister and I received plenty of discipline while growing up. She also had quite a formidable temper when she was younger, although she may strongly object to this historical description. Her daughters remember an angry young woman. She may remember herself rather differently. Some family disputes are probably beyond the reach of even spiritual philosophy.

What I understand much better today is what existed underneath that strictness. My mother has always believed that loving someone includes correcting them when they are wrong. She did it when we were children and she still does it now. When my emotions cloud my judgment, she brings me back to perspective. When I become too judgmental about someone, she corrects me. When she thinks I am wrong, she does not modify her opinion simply because I am her daughter. She tells me what she believes is right. Sometimes I agree with her immediately, sometimes I argue, and sometimes I understand much later that she saw something I could not see at the time.

A mother is often described as a child's first teacher. Mine has never retired from that role. She continues to guide me, question me, ground me and remind me that affection and correction are not opposites. Sometimes the people who love us most are also the people willing to tell us what we least want to hear.

She has been a devoted wife, a deeply loving daughter, an attached and caring sister and an extraordinarily dedicated mother. Family has shaped some of the largest decisions of her life. She placed professional advancement aside for it, organized much of her life around it and continues to worry about everyone in it. So much of what she gave was never announced as sacrifice. She simply gave because that is how she loves.

I also want something to be very clear about her presence on The Cosmic Pattern. Prameela is not writing here simply because she is my mother. She belongs here in her own right. She has spent decades reading India's epics and spiritual literature, living within traditional practices and reflecting seriously on faith, dharma, relationships and human character. She is a strong writer with a natural command of Telugu and with the kind of perspective that comes from a lifetime of reading, observing, experiencing, questioning and thinking. Her understanding does not need to mirror mine. In fact, part of what I look forward to is allowing our different voices and interpretations to exist beside one another.

Her articles on The Cosmic Pattern will carry her reflections, her understanding and her authorship. She may write about Krishna, Karna and the many other characters and questions that have stayed with her through years of spiritual reading. Her original articles will be written in Telugu, the language in which she writes with the greatest natural depth, and I will translate them into English while trying to preserve the meaning, emotion and richness of her own voice.

Writing about her makes me realize how much of a mother's life children understand only in retrospect. When we are young, we simply experience our mother being our mother. We do not calculate the promotions she declined, the career she might have built, the afternoons she could not leave work even though she wished she could, the guilt she carried because her child once said, “You don't love me,” the money she still saves, the books she continues to read, the prayers she performs, the discipline we resisted or the countless ordinary decisions through which her family came before herself. What looks ordinary to a child can reveal itself, years later, as a lifetime of extraordinary love.

Today, when my mother remembers that she could not pick me up from school and still feels guilty, I wish I could somehow give that younger version of both of us the understanding I have now. I would tell teenage me that love is not measured by who is standing outside the school gate at three in the afternoon. Sometimes love is a mother sitting at work because she is carrying responsibilities her child cannot yet understand. Sometimes it is declining another promotion. Sometimes it is correcting you when you are wrong. Sometimes it is carrying spiritual books across an ocean. And sometimes, even decades later, it is choosing the idli because some part of her still wants to save the rest for her children.

My mother has been my rock, my teacher, my critic, my source of strength and one of the people in my life whose love I have never had to question. The older I become, the more I understand that some of the things I once complained about were themselves expressions of everything she was trying to hold together for us.

Perhaps that is why I find myself emotional while writing this page. I can describe her career, her intelligence, her spirituality, her love of books, her discipline and the sacrifices she made. I can write about the professional, wife, daughter, sister, mother, reader and spiritual seeker that she has been. But none of those descriptions completely captures the person I know simply as Amma.

The older I grow, the more I understand that no achievement, no title and no words could ever measure what my Amma has given me. I can only hope that this page carries a small part of the love, gratitude and respect I feel for her.
